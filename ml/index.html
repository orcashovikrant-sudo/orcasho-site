Orcasho Scientific Solutions — Technical Whitepaper

A model that learns from your own patients, not the internet's.

How Orcasho turns years of real prescriptions and real patient feedback into a private, self-improving system that recommends the next supplement combination — and shows exactly why.

Prepared for: clinical, scientific & investment review Scope: data pipeline, model design, validation, roadmap
01

Executive summary

In one sentence: Orcasho has built a system that reads its own historical prescriptions and patient feedback, turns them into a structured record of what was given and what happened, and uses that record to predict what to try next for a new patient — entirely on a single computer, with no data ever leaving the practice.

Everything in this document is one half of a larger engine: the real-world evidence layer, F9, inside Orcasho's full nine-factor scoring architecture. See Section 08 for how it combines with a first-principles ionic-chemistry model, F1–F8.

Most healthcare software either stores records passively (an EHR) or predicts from population-scale public datasets that have never seen this practice's patients. Orcasho's model does neither. It is trained exclusively on this practice's own history: real prescriptions written by real doctors, and real outcomes reported by real patients over roughly three years, largely through ordinary WhatsApp check-ins.

The result is a system that, given a new patient's symptoms, can suggest a supplement combination, predict how each symptom is likely to respond, and show the real historical cases — this patient's own past visits, and other patients with similar profiles — that the suggestion is based on. Every new prescription and every new piece of feedback makes the system slightly more accurate, automatically.

02

The problem

Clinics generate exactly the data needed to personalize care — and then almost never use it.

Every consultation produces two things: a decision (what was prescribed) and a result (how the patient responded). Over years and hundreds of patients, that adds up to a genuine record of cause and effect. In practice, though, this record usually just sits in folders and chat threads. Nobody prescribes based on it, because nobody has turned it into something usable — and doctors have neither the time nor the tooling to comb back through years of WhatsApp messages before every consultation.

Generic health AI tools don't solve this. They're trained on population studies or public datasets that have never seen this specific practice's patients, its specific supplement combinations, or its specific doctors' judgment. They can tell you what's true on average across a broad population. They cannot tell you what has actually worked, for this practice's actual patients, with this practice's actual protocols.

Orcasho's premise is simple: the most relevant training data for predicting how your next patient will respond is how your last thousand patients responded.

03

Where the data comes from

Nothing was collected specifically for this project. The model is built entirely from records the practice already had.

Prescriptions

1,317 PDF prescriptions spanning three visual formats and, for scanned documents, optical character recognition (OCR). Each is parsed into patient name, date, supplement combination, dose, and timing.

Feedback

Patient check-ins collected two ways: free-text messages inside a WhatsApp consultation group (1,093 entries), and structured 6-day and 20-day follow-up notes saved as PDFs (37 more entries recovered separately).

This is realistic clinical data, not a clean laboratory dataset — and that is treated as a feature to design around, not a flaw to hide. WhatsApp threads mix genuine patient feedback with ordinary staff conversation. Prescriptions were written across three different templates over three years, some scanned rather than typed. Patient names are spelled inconsistently by different family members and staff. A production system for real clinics has to be built to tolerate exactly this kind of mess, and the pipeline described below exists mainly to do that safely.

04

The pipeline: from raw files to a training set

Six stages turn unstructured documents into one clean table the model can learn from — each stage checked against real output before moving to the next.

1 — Extraction

Every prescription PDF is read directly where possible, and passed through OCR where it was scanned rather than typed. Three separate document templates are recognized and parsed into the same structured record: patient, date, supplements, dose, timing.

2 — Scoring

Every feedback message is converted into a number, 1 to 10, for each of 12 symptom domains — described fully in the next section.

3 — Linking

Each scored feedback entry is matched to the specific prescription it is responding to, using patient name and the closest date. Where a prescription's own date was missing, it is placed between that same patient's known surrounding visits rather than guessed at randomly.

4 — Identity reconciliation

Real clinical records are never spelled consistently. The same patient can appear as "Vijaylakshmi," "vijaylaxmi," and "vijaya laxhmi" across different documents — while a completely different patient might coincidentally be named something that looks almost identical, such as "Sneha" and "Neha." Getting this wrong silently merges two different people's medical histories, which is worse than leaving the record unmatched. This stage resolves genuine spelling variants back into one identity, using a human-reviewed mapping file rather than an automatic similarity score — because, as tested directly on this dataset, no similarity threshold reliably tells "same person, different spelling" apart from "different people, similar names." 925 patient names now resolve automatically; every uncertain case was confirmed by hand before being merged, and nothing was merged on a guess.

5 — Careful reuse of the unlinked feedback

Some feedback never finds a matching prescription at all — for instance if the prescription itself was never captured. Rather than discarding it, each such entry is compared, by outcome pattern, to the most similar real, verified case, and the treatment from that closest match is borrowed as a plausible stand-in. These rows are permanently tagged as inferred rather than confirmed, so the distinction is never lost, and every model report states how many rows were real versus inferred.

05

The scoring engine

A transparent, rule-based reader — not a black-box language model — turns each patient's own words into a number.

Each feedback message is split into sentences. Any sentence mentioning one of 12 tracked symptom domains — reflux, bloating, sleep, energy, and so on — is classified into one of four outcomes: resolved, improved, unchanged, or worse, based on the specific language used ("no longer," "much better," "still there," "worse than before"). That classification, adjusted for whether the issue was described as mild or frequent, produces a 1–10 score for that domain in that visit.

Why rule-based, not a large language model

A rule-based engine is fully auditable: for any score, you can point to the exact sentence and the exact rule that produced it. In a clinical context, where scores feed directly into what gets recommended to a real patient, that traceability is treated as more valuable than the marginal accuracy a opaque language model might add. It also means the scoring never silently drifts as an external model provider changes something outside the practice's control.

The engine also encodes domain-specific clinical logic directly: for example, a patient who reports coming off a long-term antacid entirely is scored as meaningfully improved on reflux even if their described symptoms sound moderate, because discontinuing the medication is itself strong evidence of a durable improvement.

06

The predictive model

Eleven separate models — one per symptom domain — each trained to answer one question: given this supplement combination and this patient's own recent history, what score is this domain likely to land at?

In Orcasho's full nine-factor architecture, this is the F9 layer — see Section 08 for how it combines with the eight mechanistic ionic factors, F1–F8.

What kind of model, and why

Each domain is predicted by a random forest — a model built from many simple decision trees, each trained on a slightly different random slice of the data, whose individual predictions are then averaged together. It was chosen deliberately over more fashionable alternatives, for reasons specific to this data:

  • It performs well with modest data. The largest domain has under 900 labeled visits; the smallest has 15. Deep learning models typically need orders of magnitude more examples to avoid simply memorizing the data; random forests remain reliable at this scale.
  • It resists overfitting almost by construction. Because each tree only ever sees part of the data and part of the features, no single quirky patient record can dominate the final prediction.
  • It is explainable. It is possible to inspect which features — which supplements, which prior scores — actually drove a given prediction, which matters enormously for clinical trust and is largely unavailable in deep neural networks.
  • It handles messy, mixed data natively. The inputs mix yes/no supplement flags, continuous prior scores, and counts, without needing careful rescaling or preprocessing.

What the model actually sees

For every visit, the model is given: which of the 126 known supplements were included in the combination; the patient's own most recently known score in every one of the 12 domains (their personal baseline); the total daily pill count; and which visit number this is for that patient (an early proxy for how long they have been in treatment). It is trained to predict that visit's actual reported outcome.

Validated honestly, not just reported

Every domain model is evaluated with five-fold cross-validation: the data is split five ways, the model is trained on four-fifths and tested on the held-out fifth, five times over, so that its accuracy is always measured on visits it never trained on. That accuracy is then compared against the simplest possible baseline — always predicting the domain's average score — because a model is only useful if it beats that baseline. The full comparison is in Section 09.

07

Case-based reasoning: showing the evidence

A prediction without evidence is a black box. Orcasho pairs its predictions with the actual historical cases they're based on.

This evidence — real cases, real outcomes — is exactly what F9 draws on, via the Section 06 model, for every compound. See Section 08.

Alongside the random forest predictions, every patient's current profile is compared — using nearest-neighbor search over their symptom scores and supplement history — against every other visit ever recorded, split deliberately into two groups:

This patient's own history

How they personally responded to past combinations — the single strongest signal available for what might work next.

Other similar patients

Different patients whose symptom pattern closely resembles this one, and what was tried for them.

This mirrors how an experienced clinician actually reasons — "this reminds me of a case I saw" — rather than asking staff to trust a number with no story behind it. In an early technical test, a long-history patient's nearest neighbors were, unhelpfully, entirely their own past visits; the system was corrected specifically so both comparisons are always shown, not just whichever is statistically closest.

08

The complete engine: F1–F9

Everything described so far — the pipeline, the scoring engine, the random forest, case-based reasoning — is one input into a larger nine-factor formula. F1–F8 are a first-principles ionic-chemistry model of what should work. F9 is this document's evidence layer: what has actually worked. Every supplement's final score is the two, multiplied and blended.

What changed in this build

Every factor that used to read a raw prescription-frequency count — F2's severity amplifier, F7's empirical weight, and F9's fallback score — now reads the trained predictive model from Section 06 instead. There is no longer a separate "how often was this written" signal sitting apart from "how well did this actually work." Prescriptions and feedback were always both needed just to train that model in the first place (Sections 03–05); now that it exists and is validated out-of-sample (Section 09), it is the single source those three factors draw from, for every compound, every time — not a fallback that only kicks in once someone manually types in an outcome.

The 15-ion state space

Every patient's symptom scores are converted into a target vector across 15 biochemical markers — the ionic and metabolic axes gut function actually runs on. Every supplement in the library has its own fixed 15-dimensional vector, describing which way it pushes each axis. Four of the fifteen carry most of the mechanistic weight:

H⁺
Enzyme switch

Pepsin needs pH <3.0; pancreatic enzymes need pH 6.5–7.5. Dysregulated H⁺ misfires digestion before absorption ever begins.

Cl⁻
Acid + fluid control

HCl is H⁺ paired with Cl⁻. PPIs suppress H⁺ but never correct Cl⁻ — one reason acid-suppression alone rebounds.

Na⁺
Absorption controller

SGLT1, NHE3, and bile-acid transporters are all sodium-dependent; Na⁺ disruption cascades into broader malabsorption.

ROS
Downstream saboteur

Instability upstream leaks into mitochondrial superoxide production — correcting the ions above tends to bring ROS down as a consequence, not a separate target.

The remaining eleven markers — HCO₃⁻, K⁺, Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺, NH₄⁺, CO₂, pH buffer, O₂⁻, Zn²⁺, Fe²⁺, Mn²⁺ — follow the same logic: each has a defined physiological role, and each condition (GERD, constipation, bloating) has its own target signature across all 15, defined separately at five severity bands.

A simplified 8-spoke cutaway of the real 15-axis space. The shaded polygon is one condition's target at one severity band — where the patient's ionic state needs to move toward. Every supplement in the library is a vector plotted the same way.

F1 — direction, and only direction

F1 is pure cosine similarity between a compound's vector and the patient's (medication-adjusted) target vector — it answers exactly one question: does this compound push the right way? No strength or dose term lives here; a compound can point perfectly at the target and still score near zero overall if F2–F8 say it's too weak, unsafe, or unproven for this severity.

F2–F8 — the seven-part magnitude stack

F1 answers "which direction." These seven answer "how strongly, and how appropriately."

F2
Severity amplifier

At high total severity, compounds the Section 06 model rates as reliably effective are amplified; at low severity, safety is favoured instead.

F3
Symptom specificity

Weighted match to whichever of GERD, constipation, and bloating are actually active for this patient.

F4
Mechanism depth

Compounds correcting an upstream ionic root are favoured over ones that only quiet a symptom — and more so as severity rises.

F5
Mucosal repair

Once any domain crosses a barrier-breach threshold, repair-indexed compounds (e.g. L-Glutamine, Zinc Carnosine) are activated.

F6
Safety cap

Potency can't override patient safety — unsafe compounds are actively penalised as severity climbs, not just left unboosted.

F7
Empirical weight × potency

The Section 06 model's own predicted effectiveness — not a hand-set literature guess, and no longer a raw frequency count — blended with the compound's raw ionic strength.

F8
Medication correction bonus

Up to +40% for compounds that directly replenish an ion a patient's medication is actively depleting.

Medications as targeted corrections

Each medication in the library carries its own 15-dimensional depletion vector — a PPI, for instance, is encoded as strongly suppressing H⁺ while depleting Mg²⁺, Ca²⁺, Zn²⁺, and Fe²⁺. That vector shifts the patient's target toward demanding more correction on exactly those axes, and F8 rewards any compound that replenishes them. A statin patient's target quietly amplifies CoQ10; a diuretic patient's amplifies potassium and magnesium correction — without a clinician having to remember to prescribe for the side effect separately.

F9 — where the rest of this whitepaper lives

F9 is real-world usage priority, and it is the single biggest bridge between this section and everything in Sections 02–07. Earlier builds gave F9 a fallback: when no outcome data existed yet for a compound, its raw score came from simple prescription-frequency and co-prescription counts — a proxy for how often something was written, not how well it worked. That fallback is retired. With the random-forest model in Section 06 trained and cross-validated (Section 09) on the full set of prescription–outcome pairs, F9's raw score for every compound, every time, is that model's own predicted outcome for this specific patient's actual profile — not a count of how often the compound has been prescribed in the past.

The blend weight is dynamic, not fixed

F9's share of the final score still moves between 35% and 65% — that range is unchanged, and for the same reason as before: F2 and F7 already pull the model's signal into the mechanistic composite, so weighting F9 at a flat 50% on top of that would double-count the same evidence. What changed is what drives the movement. It no longer flips on a binary "has a clinician typed in an outcome yet?" switch. It now scales continuously with that symptom domain's own cross-validated reliability from Section 09 — a domain like reflux or bloating, which clears the baseline comfortably, earns F9 a share close to the 65% ceiling; a domain like diarrhea or appetite, which hasn't reliably beaten baseline yet, is held down near the 35% floor until more visits arrive. The model is already honest about its own confidence per domain; the blend now listens to that directly instead of waiting on a manual entry.

35% F9 / 65% composite, at a domain's least-validated → 65% F9 / 35% composite, at a domain's best-validated — the beam tips continuously with Section 09's own cross-validated accuracy per domain, not with a one-time manual entry.

Why two layers, not one

What F1–F8 alone can't do

A pure ionic model has no memory of what actually happened to real patients — it can propose a mechanistically sound compound that, in this practice's hands, simply hasn't worked as well as the theory suggests.

What F9 alone can't do

A pure usage/outcome model has no opinion about a compound nobody has tried yet — it cannot reason from first principles about a new patient's specific ionic profile the way F1–F8 can, even with zero prior data on that exact combination.

Multiplying the two together means a compound has to be both mechanistically justified and real-world proven — or explicitly trusted more as one signal strengthens while the practice waits for the other to catch up. That is the literal mechanism behind the "mechanistic, not just predictive" claim in Section 11: F1–F8 is the traceable first-principles reasoning; F9, described in full in Sections 02–07, is the traceable evidence. Neither one is a black box on its own, and multiplying two inspectable signals keeps the product inspectable too.

10

What makes this different

Four properties, taken together, that are not found in typical healthcare software or generic health AI products.

1. It learns from this practice's own outcomes, not population averages

Most clinical decision-support tools are trained once, on public or licensed population data, and then sold identically to every clinic. This system's entire training set is this practice's own three years of prescriptions and patient-reported outcomes. It cannot be purchased pre-built by a competitor, because the thing that makes it accurate is this practice's specific history.

2. Every new visit makes it slightly smarter, automatically

There is no separate "data labeling" step and no waiting for the next model release. A new prescription and its feedback are scored and folded into the training set the same day, and retraining the full model takes seconds. The system compounds by simply continuing to operate.

3. It shows its work

Every recommendation is accompanied by the specific historical cases behind it — this patient's own past visits and other similar patients — rather than asking anyone to trust a number with no explanation. Combined with a fully auditable, rule-based scoring engine, every score and every recommendation can be traced back to the real sentence and the real prior case that produced it.

4. It was built for the data that actually exists, not the data that should exist

Nearly every clinical AI system assumes clean, structured electronic health records as an input. This one was built to read three different prescription formats, scanned documents, a WhatsApp group chat mixing staff banter with genuine patient feedback, and inconsistent name spellings — because that is what real, working small-practice records actually look like. The tolerance for that mess is not an afterthought; it is most of the engineering.

See Section 11 for a direct, feature-by-feature comparison against EHRs, population health-AI, and consumer health apps.

12

Local-first and private by construction

The entire system — extraction, scoring, training, prediction, and the interface used to view it — runs on a single computer. No patient data, and no part of the practice's prescribing logic, is ever sent to an external server.

This was a deliberate architectural decision, not a limitation worked around later. Every script reads files from, and writes files back to, the same local folder. The one point where an external AI service was used at all — the conversation that helped build this system — never had the underlying patient-identifiable dataset uploaded to it; the pipeline was developed and validated locally, using the practice's own machine, from the start.

For a practice whose treatment protocols and patient outcome history are commercially sensitive, this matters as much as any modeling choice described above: the intellectual property this document describes stays entirely within the practice that built it.

13

The learning loop, end to end

How a single new patient interaction moves through the whole system.

  1. A doctor writes a new prescription; it is saved as a PDF like every other.
  2. Days later, the patient sends feedback — by WhatsApp or a structured follow-up note.
  3. The scoring engine reads that feedback and produces 12 domain scores.
  4. The visit is linked to its prescription and added to the training table.
  5. On request, all eleven domain models retrain on the updated table — a matter of seconds at the current data size.
  6. The next time that patient, or a similar one, is seen, the system can suggest a combination, predict its likely effect per domain, and show the real cases behind that suggestion.
14

Honest limitations

A credible model is one that states plainly where it is weak.

Where this system should not yet be trusted blindly

  • Two of twelve domains (diarrhea, appetite) do not yet clearly outperform a naive guess, purely due to low sample size. They will improve as more data accumulates and should be weighted accordingly today.
  • About 10% of training rows are inferred, not confirmed — feedback borrowed from the closest similar real case because no matching prescription could be found. These are clearly tagged in the data and can be excluded entirely with a single setting if a more conservative, real-data-only model is ever preferred.
  • Roughly 120 prescriptions still have no linked feedback at all — most likely because the patient never replied to that specific cycle — so those combinations exist in the system's candidate pool without direct outcome evidence yet.
  • This model recommends; it does not diagnose or override clinical judgment. Every output is intended as a decision-support signal for a qualified practitioner, not a replacement for one.
15

Why this model, in depth

The full case for random forests as the engine, why that choice is reliable today, and where the training approach goes as the data grows.

Why random forest beats the fashionable alternatives, here

vs. deep learning / neural nets

Deep nets need thousands of examples per class to generalize instead of memorize. The smallest Orcasho domain has 15 visits, the largest under 900. At this scale a deep net overfits; a forest of shallow trees, each seeing a random slice, does not.

vs. a large language model

An LLM scoring or predicting outcomes is a black box that can drift whenever its vendor updates it, and cannot point to the exact rule behind a number. A regulator, investor, or doctor asking "why this score" gets an answer only from a rule-based engine and an inspectable tree.

vs. a single decision tree

One tree memorizes quirks in whichever patients it happened to see. Averaging many trees, each trained on a different random slice of visits and features, cancels out that noise — the textbook reason ensembles beat single models on small, noisy, real-world data.

vs. classic linear/logistic regression

Regression assumes each supplement's effect adds up independently. Real combinations interact — one supplement's effect can depend on what else is in the mix. Trees capture that interaction natively; linear models have to be told about it in advance.

Why it's reliable, not just convenient

  • Every number is validated out-of-sample. Five-fold cross-validation means no domain's reported accuracy ever comes from data the model was trained on.
  • Every number is compared to a null result. A model that can't beat "always guess the average" is reported as unreliable, not hidden — see Section 09.
  • Variance is structurally reduced. Bootstrap sampling and random feature selection across trees is the mechanism, not a marketing claim — it's why the model degrades gracefully instead of catastrophically as sample size in a domain shrinks.
  • Failure is legible. Because the model is a forest of readable trees rather than millions of opaque weights, a low-confidence domain (diarrhea, appetite) can be traced directly to sample size, not chalked up to an unexplainable model quirk.

Where the training approach goes from here

Now — ~1,100 visits

Random forest, per symptom domain

Optimized for exactly this data regime: modest volume, high noise tolerance, full explainability required.

Next — thousands of visits

Gradient-boosted trees as a candidate upgrade

Same explainability profile as a forest, typically sharper accuracy once each domain clears a few thousand labeled visits — a swap-in, not a rebuild, since the training table stays identical.

Later — enough volume per combination

Causal / uplift modeling

Move from "what score is likely" to "how much did this specific combination change the score versus doing nothing" — the step from correlation toward attributable effect, once enough patients have comparable histories to estimate it honestly.

Longer term

Federated learning across practices, without pooling patient data

Multiple practices could each keep training locally and share only model updates, not records — preserving the local-first guarantee in Section 12 while letting the mechanistic patterns compound across more than one clinic's history.

What this means for healthcare infrastructure more broadly

Every clinic that has been operating for a few years is sitting on the same untapped asset: a real record of decisions and outcomes. Almost none of it is used, because turning messy prescriptions and WhatsApp threads into a trainable table is unglamorous engineering, not a modeling breakthrough. Orcasho's pipeline is a template for that unglamorous step — repeatable at any practice with its own history, without requiring that history to ever leave the building. At larger scale, and only with deliberate, consented aggregation, the same bottom-up approach could start doing something population studies structurally cannot: surfacing which supplement combinations mechanistically track with which symptom resolutions, built up from thousands of small, real, practice-level cause-and-effect records rather than one large trial testing one variable at a time. That is a different shape of evidence than a randomized controlled trial — narrower in certainty, but far closer to how supplements are actually prescribed and combined in practice, and available years before an equivalent formal trial could be funded, run, and published.

Glossary

Every technical term used above — what it means, and why that specific choice was made for this system.

Random forest

A prediction method built from many simple decision trees, each trained on a slightly different random sample of the data; their individual guesses are averaged for a final answer that is more stable than any single tree.

Why here: it is the one model family that is simultaneously accurate at low sample sizes, explainable tree-by-tree, and unbothered by mixed yes/no and numeric inputs — see Section 15 for the full comparison against neural nets, LLMs, and regression.

Mean Absolute Error (MAE)

The average size of the gap between a model's prediction and the real outcome, measured in the same units as the thing being predicted — here, points on a 1–10 symptom scale.

Why here: MAE is easy to explain to a clinician in plain terms ("off by about 1.5 points on average") and doesn't over-punish the occasional large miss the way squared-error metrics do — important with domains as small as 15 visits.

Cross-validation

A way of testing a model fairly: split the data into chunks, train on most of them, test on the chunk left out, and repeat until every chunk has had a turn being the test set.

Why here: without it, every reported accuracy number would be inflated by the model having already seen the answer — five-fold validation is the minimum bar for a number in this document to be trustworthy.

Baseline

The simplest possible comparison point — here, a "model" that always predicts the domain's overall average score, regardless of the patient. Any real model needs to beat this to be worth using.

Why here: it's the honesty check in Section 09 — it's how diarrhea and appetite were caught as not-yet-reliable instead of quietly reported as working.

Nearest-neighbor / similarity search

Finding the historical cases whose numeric profile is mathematically closest to a new patient's, the same way you might say two shapes look "close" on a graph.

Why here: it's what turns a bare number into a shown case — the mechanism behind Section 07's "this reminds me of a patient I saw" reasoning.

OCR (Optical Character Recognition)

Software that reads text out of a scanned image or photo of a document, so it can be processed the same way as a typed file.

Why here: a meaningful share of the 1,317 prescriptions were scanned, not typed — without OCR, that history would simply be unusable.

Feature

Any single piece of information given to the model to help it predict — for example, "was Probiotics in the combination" or "what was this patient's last sleep score."

Why here: which features drove a prediction is exactly what's inspectable in a random forest and exactly what's hidden in a neural net — the basis of the explainability claim in Section 06 and 15.

Imputed / inferred data

A row in the training data where the actual treatment was not directly recorded, so the most similar real, verified case's treatment was borrowed as a reasonable stand-in — always clearly labeled as such.

Why here: roughly 10% of rows carry this tag; it exists so anyone can filter them out and rerun on real-data-only if a more conservative model is ever preferred — nothing is silently blended.

Mechanistic vs. correlational

Correlational: "patients like this tended to improve." Mechanistic, as used in this document: every step from raw sentence to final suggestion is a specific, inspectable rule or split — not a claim about biochemical pathways.

Why here: the word "mechanistic" is doing real work in Section 11 — it's the property most population health-AI and LLM-based tools cannot offer, because their internals aren't inspectable at that resolution.
