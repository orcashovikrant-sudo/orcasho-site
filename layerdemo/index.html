How the model works, step by step

This page is a simplified view of the full Orcasho system. The real engine works with 15-dimensional ionic vectors and four pH-defined digestive gates. It can evaluate many candidate combinations, including thousands of possible combinations, and compare the resulting simulations to identify which direction is closest to the target.

1 · Define the targetFirst define the state we want to move toward. In this example, the stomach target is lower H+ and higher HCO3−.
2 · Represent each compoundEach compound is represented as a vector. Here we use only two dimensions: its effect on H+ and HCO3−.
3 · Move the compoundMove the H+ and HCO3− controls to change the direction and magnitude of that compound's chemical effect. The arrow moves with it.
4 · Compare to the targetCosine similarity compares the compound's direction with the target direction. A higher positive cosine means closer directional alignment.
5 · Combine compoundsGive Compound A and Compound B different weights. The system calculates a weighted combination, creating a new vector and therefore a new arrow.
6 · Run the simulationIn the full model, the candidate combination is propagated through the four digestive gates, where pH-dependent chemistry changes the resulting vector at each gate.
7 · Repeat across candidatesMany different combinations and weightings can be simulated. The resulting arrows and cosine scores can be compared against the same target.
8 · Compare and refineYou can compare two combinations, two simulation runs, or many candidate combinations and identify which one produces the stronger target-aligned direction.
What is being compared? Combination 1 → simulate → target alignment Combination 2 → simulate → target alignment Many candidates → simulate → rank by directional alignment

Important: the arrows shown below are a teaching representation. The actual system performs the same directional logic across the full 15-dimensional ionic vectors and the four digestive gates. The point of this page is to make the mechanism visible without forcing someone to stare at 15-dimensional mathematics like a punishment.

  1. Target: define the chemical direction we want the system to move toward.

  6. Comparison: repeat the same process for many candidate combinations and compare their simulated target alignment.
