The chemistry behind the combination

The whole idea in one minute

The digestive tract behaves as an ordered sequence of four chemical environments: the stomach, the duodenum (first part of the small intestine), the remainder of the small intestine, and the colon. Each is defined by its pH, its dominant ions and the charge states they produce, and the output of each stage is the input of the next. A change at an upstream stage therefore alters the conditions that every later stage has to work with.

Supplements do not replace this system. They act within the body's own buffering salts (bicarbonate, phosphate, amino acids), which set the pH at each stage. The combination is chosen so that every stage has supporting chemistry.

How upstream causes downstream

Three familiar kinds of cause are chained together. Each is textbook physiology on its own. The model is that our prescriptions follow this chain.

Signal cause

Acid arrives, so base and bile follow

Acid in the duodenum releases a hormone (secretin) that makes the pancreas release bicarbonate. Fat and protein release another hormone (cholecystokinin) that releases bile. The upstream event is the direct trigger.

Chemical cause

The pH change flips charges and solubility

Acids become charged, minerals approach their solubility limit, oils need bile. Whether an ingredient is absorbed depends on the pH it meets and on what is holding it (its ligand).

Biological cause

What is not absorbed feeds microbes

Gut microbes turn leftovers into acids, ammonium and bile-acid products. These shift the colon's pH and send signals back upstream, so the loop closes.

Every new 20-day prescription returns to this same chemistry. Patient feedback then tells us how the chemistry behaved in that particular woman, and refines the next combination.

Why a combination, not a single supplement

The important distinction is not that one supplement is “stronger” than another. It is that an orally consumed molecule enters a changing chemical environment. Orcasho therefore models the patient state first and the supplement combination second.

Example flow: iron in pregnancy

Iron is a useful example because the tablet is not the endpoint. The clinically relevant question is how much iron becomes chemically available, crosses the intestinal epithelium and enters circulation. Non-haem iron is predominantly absorbed in the duodenum and proximal small intestine. Ferric iron (Fe³⁺) is reduced to ferrous iron (Fe²⁺), which can enter enterocytes through DMT1; iron can then be stored as ferritin or exported through ferroportin and loaded onto transferrin for systemic transport.

1 · IntakeFood or prescribed iron enters with the meal and the patient's other medicines/supplements.
2 · StomachGastric acid helps keep non-haem iron chemically available and supports conversion toward Fe²⁺. Low gastric acidity or acid suppression can reduce non-haem iron absorption.
3 · DuodenumDcytb reduces Fe³⁺ to Fe²⁺; DMT1 transports Fe²⁺ into the enterocyte.
4 · Enterocyte → bloodIron is stored in ferritin or exported by ferroportin; hephaestin helps load circulating iron onto transferrin.
5 · PregnancySystemic iron contributes to maternal haemoglobin and the increased physiological demands of pregnancy. Iron requirements rise during pregnancy.

Where we fit when the patient also has GI problems

A woman with irregular periods may simultaneously have reflux, bloating, constipation, metformin-related GI symptoms, low iron/B12/folate status, or thyroid medication in the background. The clinical state is therefore not one-dimensional.

Metformin commonly causes nausea, diarrhoea and abdominal discomfort, and long-term use can be associated with vitamin B12 deficiency. Orcasho's role is not to tell the patient to stop metformin. It is to record the medication, observe GI tolerance and nutritional markers, and adapt the supportive combination around the real patient state.

Likewise, we do not claim to “replace” a progestin or combined oral contraceptive. Patient feedback about more regular cycles is treated as an observed pattern requiring further clinical validation, not as proof that the supplement programme reproduces the pharmacological effect of hormonal therapy.
