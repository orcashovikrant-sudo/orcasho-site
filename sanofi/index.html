01B · Why the small intestine

For women, health runs through absorption.

Iron, magnesium, calcium and B12 are taken up in the stomach and small intestine. When acid-reducing medicines, a changing cycle, pregnancy or cancer care disturb that part of the digestive tract, the nutrients women need most can be affected first.

Starting pointReflux, bloating, constipation

Common across a woman’s life, and often treated with acid-reducing medicines for years.

→
What followsLess stomach acid

Long-term PPI use can lower absorption of calcium, magnesium and vitamin B12. The evidence on iron is mixed.

→
Where it landsA body with higher needs

Women of reproductive age already face high demand for iron and other nutrients, and more in pregnancy.

30.7%

of women aged 15 to 49 had anaemia in 2023

WHO estimate. It rises to 35.5% in pregnant women. Anaemia has several causes, and iron uptake in the small intestine is one of the routes our loop follows over time.

Why a 20-day loop suits this

Hormones, pregnancy, postpartum recovery and cancer treatment change digestion from month to month. A fixed plan drifts. Orcasho re-reads the patient every cycle, with clinicians reviewing, and no diet restrictions.

02 · Our approach

The small intestine is a critical interface.

The model connects digestive chemistry, small-intestinal function, nutrient absorption and observed response. Each 20-day cycle adds evidence to the patient’s longitudinal profile.

The core idea

Move from one-time recommendations to a feedback loop.

Biochemical hypotheses become more useful when connected to real-world observations, clinician review and measured response.

Small intestineAbsorptionSymptomsBlood markers20-day cycles

Chronic care usually treats small-intestinal dysfunction as a symptom to suppress. We treat it as a control variable to manage continuously, so that absorption can be tuned and re-checked rather than assumed.

The gap between visits

The patient spends 340 days a year outside the health system.

Moving the small intestine from treatment target to control variable is what makes a feedback loop possible where a static protocol never can. That loop has to run in the 340 days, not the 25.

02B · Beyond digestion

Small intestine health, fertility, and pregnancy.

Optimising villi function in the small intestine improves absorption of zinc, magnesium, iron, folate and B12 - nutrients directly tied to hormone synthesis (FSH, LH, estrogen, progesterone), menstrual regularity, ovulation quality, and pregnancy outcomes including implantation, placental health, and fetal growth. As reported by patients preparing for pregnancy, the same pH and enzyme groundwork that resolves gut symptoms also supports reproductive health.

MENSTRUAL

Hormone synthesis support

Increases plasma nutrient levels to support FSH, LH, estrogen, progesterone - promoting regular cycles, reducing menstrual pain and heavy bleeding via magnesium (relaxes uterine muscles) and iron (prevents anemia). Folate and B12 improve period quality, reducing fatigue.

FERTILITY

Ovarian function and egg quality

Zinc and magnesium absorption enhances ovarian function and egg quality, supports ovulation timing and gamete health, and reduces inflammation from PCOS or endometriosis - improving uterine receptivity.

PREGNANCY

Implantation through fetal growth

Nutrient-rich blood supply enhances embryo development and implantation, improves placental health, reduces preeclampsia risk, and promotes healthy fetal growth, higher live birth rates, and proper pregnancy spacing and structural fetal development.

04 · Why rural health

Continuity of care across rural distances.

Women’s health meets two rural barriers: distance and limited access to primary and specialty care. Orcasho’s model adds structured check-ins between visits, built with local clinics and community health partners.

The two barriers we address

  • Access to care and distance: routine check-ins and protocols delivered at home, no clinic trip.
  • Access to primary care and specialty providers: structured, clinician-reviewed follow-up between appointments.

Local partnership

  • Work with a U.S. rural clinic, community health center or women’s-health provider.
  • Adapt the workflow with local patients and clinicians.
  • Agree on referral and escalation pathways.
  • Track engagement, follow-up completion and patient experience.

Plugging into primary care.

06D · Outcomes

Every woman has a target from day one.

Care starts from where she is. Her starting state is recorded at intake, and her target is set from it.

INTAKEStarting state recorded: medicines, doses, conditions, food pattern, and markers where available.
TARGETSet from that starting state, for example PPI use down within a set number of days.
EVERY 20 DAYSFeedback read against the target, medicine use and symptoms logged.
HIT OR MISSRecorded for every patient, above or below the target.
NEXT CYCLEThe protocol adjusts. Medicine changes stay with the doctor.

What a target looks like

Examples by starting point, not results.

  • Menstrual health and PCOS/PCOD: cycle regularity, bloating and constipation scores, medicine use.
  • Pregnancy and postpartum: symptom scores and markers where available, with targets agreed with her clinician.
  • During and after chemotherapy: markers such as TSH, lipids and albumin, and tolerance of food and medicines.

Who is counted

Every woman in the cohort, including those who miss the target or land above or below it. Patients pay for outcomes, so a missed target shows up in renewals.

  • Tracked: PPI, antacid, laxative and metformin use, symptoms and markers.
  • Medicine changes are decided by the doctor, never the model.
  • In the 12-week pilot, targets are agreed with the local clinician before kickoff.

Why patients keep renewing, and where it leaks

Why patients keep renewing

  • Already stable: symptoms have largely resolved. They continue for the freedom to eat normally again, or to protect absorption of chronic-care medications, visible in their blood markers.
  • 80 to 90% healed: meaningful improvement, not full resolution. They continue to close the remaining gap to stability.

Where churn comes from

  • Healed: symptoms have resolved. They no longer feel a need to continue.
  • Drop off mid-cycle: stop partway through, with no stated reason.
  • Financial strain: unable to continue for cost reasons. Likely underrepresented, since some "no stated reason" drop-offs are financial too.

08 · Bringing it to the U.S.

What carries over. What we adapt.

What works in one country does not automatically work in another. We have mapped the U.S. requirements, and the pilot is designed around them.

Carries over

  • The 20-day loop and the patient-state model.
  • A licensed doctor reviews and signs every prescription.
  • We make, open and relabel nothing. Patients buy ordinary retail products from our list of links.
  • Patients are already served in the U.S. on this system.

We adapt for rural U.S. communities

  • Licensing: U.S. patients need clinicians licensed in their state, so the partner clinic’s own clinicians provide oversight.
  • Communication: program-level outcomes, no disease claims on product links.
  • Data: designed to the stricter of HIPAA and FTC rules.
  • Language and trust: co-designed with the community first.
