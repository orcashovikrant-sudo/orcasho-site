02, The two doors

Same engine,
two very different doors in.

The intervention engine already runs, proven through B2C in India. The question a partner will actually ask is why India is B2C and the United States is B2B. It comes down to whether a doctor has anything to refer a patient into.

01Why go from B2C in India to B2B in the US?

Because the intervention engine is already built and validated through B2C. The US has existing chronic-care infrastructure for that engine to plug into, which is a distribution decision, not a rebuild.

02Why not go B2B in India instead?

Because no B2B chronic-care workflow exists there yet for a doctor to refer into. Indian physician interactions are episodic: prescription, then the patient leaves. We became that missing layer ourselves, directly with patients.

03Why keep growing B2C at all, alongside B2B?

Because B2C in India is already working well, so there is no reason to slow it down. Every cycle it keeps compounding the longitudinal patient database that the entire US B2B story rests on. B2C keeps deepening the data here while B2B is built there, at the same time, not one before the other.

03, Why patients find us

The market doesn't have
a system. We became one.

Most patients don't arrive at Orcasho first. They arrive after two or three doctors, a stack of prescriptions that stopped working, and the growing sense that no one is actually tracking them. Indian chronic care is vertical: a doctor treats the visit in front of them, hands over medication, and the relationship ends until the patient chooses to come back. If it doesn't hold, the patient goes looking for a new doctor rather than back to the same one, and the exhaustion compounds with every switch.

Vertical care, the default

  • One prescription, one visit, then silence
  • No one owns what happens after the appointment
  • A relapse sends the patient to a new doctor, not back to the same one
  • Exhaustion compounds with every doctor switched

Longitudinal care, what we run

  • Re-assessed every ~20 days, on the same patient
  • One team owns diabetes, cholesterol, and the digestive tract together
  • Adjusts continuously, whatever the medication or diet variation
  • The patient stops needing a doctor visit to feel managed

This is what patients notice once they're in the program: someone is watching the whole picture, on a cycle, whether or not they walk into a clinic. Every ~20 days we re-check what actually changed for them.

Sleep cycles
Medication response
Bowel movements
Blood markers

Some patients still see a doctor on a schedule, post-chemo patients, or check-ins every one, two, or three quarters. We stay in the middle regardless. By the time they're back in front of that doctor, they're already stable, and it reads as the doctor's win. That's often what turns into a referral, not a pitch we made, but a patient the doctor didn't have to worry about.

None of this was built as a stated strategy. It's what happens when a patient is followed for 20 months instead of 20 minutes. India doesn't have a longitudinal chronic-care layer today. We're building one, one B2C patient at a time, and it's the same layer the US market already knows it's missing.

04, The gap

The patient spends most of the year
outside the health system.

In a typical chronic-care journey, patients may interact with their health system for roughly 25 days of the year. For the remaining ~340 days, they are living with the condition outside the clinical environment, while their symptoms, diet, medication response, absorption, and day-to-day physiology continue to change.

This is the space Orcasho is designed to operate in.

~25 days/year
~340 days/year, patient living outside the health system
Orcasho provides a continuous longitudinal intervention layer during those 340 days. Every 20 days, the patient's response is reviewed and the next intervention cycle is adjusted, with the resulting patient data continuously available to the physician.

The same pattern exists across chronic care. Patients are seen at defined clinical touchpoints, but most of their year is spent managing their condition outside the clinic. Orcasho is designed to provide the continuous monitoring, intervention, and feedback loop during that period, while keeping the physician informed and in control.

05, How the model fits

The B2C model was not built
for this gap. It just fits it.

This is the same execution layer we already run in B2C, every twenty days. The only real difference is who is watching it happen. There, it's us. Here, it's also the physician.

Value-based networks are built around a very different cadence. A patient typically visits a physician once or twice a quarter. Across the remaining days of the year, there is no structured touchpoint at all. Orcasho does not add a workflow or an administrative layer on top of that visit cadence. It moves the entire execution layer into the patient's home, runs the twenty-day intervention cycle there, and updates a physician-facing dashboard continuously, so the physician keeps full visibility and control without carrying the execution burden themselves.

Most patient care moves outside the hospital and into the home. Visit frequency drops. Physician visibility does not.
