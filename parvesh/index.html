The chemistry behind the combination

The whole idea in one minute

The digestive tract behaves as an ordered sequence of four chemical environments: the stomach, the duodenum (first part of the small intestine), the remainder of the small intestine, and the colon. Each is defined by its pH, its dominant ions and the charge states they produce, and the output of each stage is the input of the next. A change at an upstream stage therefore alters the conditions that every later stage has to work with.

Supplements do not replace this system. They act within the body's own buffering salts (bicarbonate, phosphate, amino acids), which set the pH at each stage. The combination is chosen so that every stage has supporting chemistry.

How upstream causes downstream

Three familiar kinds of cause are chained together. Each is textbook physiology on its own. The model is that our prescriptions follow this chain.

Signal cause

Acid arrives, so base and bile follow

Acid in the duodenum releases a hormone (secretin) that makes the pancreas release bicarbonate. Fat and protein release another hormone (cholecystokinin) that releases bile. The upstream event is the direct trigger.

Chemical cause

The pH change flips charges and solubility

Acids become charged, minerals approach their solubility limit, oils need bile. Whether an ingredient is absorbed depends on the pH it meets and on what is holding it (its ligand).

Biological cause

What is not absorbed feeds microbes

Gut microbes turn leftovers into acids, ammonium and bile-acid products. These shift the colon's pH and send signals back upstream, so the loop closes.

Every new 20-day prescription returns to this same chemistry. Patient feedback then tells us how the chemistry behaved in that particular patient, and refines the next combination.

Why a combination, not a single supplement

The important distinction is not that one supplement is “stronger” than another. It is that an orally consumed molecule enters a changing chemical environment. Orcasho therefore models the patient state first and the supplement combination second.

PPI dependence: the layer we build, and where your decision sits

A PPI does one job very well: it switches off the acid pump at the first gate. It does not touch the gates that follow, and a stomach that has been suppressed for months or years hands a different starting chemistry to the duodenum and the small intestine. Many patients relapse when the dose is reduced, and rebound acid hypersecretion after withdrawal has been described in the literature.

Reducing the PPI is the goal of the programme. The doctor prescribes the PPI, and we begin alongside it, with the combination built around the PPI exactly as prescribed. We do not leave it there. From the early cycles the work is aimed at reduction: the combination supports the duodenal handoff, uptake and bowel pattern that a PPI does not reach, so that the dose can come down without the rest of the tract starting unsupported. We share the response with you at each 20-day review, and every dose change is made by you, the prescribing doctor.

Example flow: iron absorption on acid suppression

Iron is a useful example because the tablet is not the endpoint. The clinically relevant question is how much iron becomes chemically available, crosses the intestinal epithelium and enters circulation. Non-haem iron is predominantly absorbed in the duodenum and proximal small intestine. Ferric iron (Fe³⁺) is reduced to ferrous iron (Fe²⁺), which can enter enterocytes through DMT1; iron can then be stored as ferritin or exported through ferroportin and loaded onto transferrin for systemic transport.

1 · IntakeFood or prescribed iron enters with the meal and the patient's other medicines/supplements.
2 · StomachGastric acid helps keep non-haem iron chemically available and supports conversion toward Fe²⁺. Low gastric acidity or acid suppression can reduce non-haem iron absorption.
3 · DuodenumDcytb reduces Fe³⁺ to Fe²⁺; DMT1 transports Fe²⁺ into the enterocyte.
4 · Enterocyte → bloodIron is stored in ferritin or exported by ferroportin; hephaestin helps load circulating iron onto transferrin.
5 · OutcomeSystemic iron contributes to haemoglobin and tissue demand. Ferritin and haemoglobin are what the clinician sees downstream.

The operating difference

Standard medication: a defined molecule, defined pharmacological target and clinically appropriate dosing schedule. The clinician individualises the prescription, but the medicine itself does not continuously rebuild its formulation from the patient's 20-day response.

Orcasho: the patient state is re-read every 20 days. The combination is then adapted from the 101-compound pool based on the observed state, medication context, GI environment, laboratory information and response history.

Not a replacement: we never ask a patient to stop or change prescribed medication on their own. For PPIs and antacids our aim is reduction, carried out by the prescribing doctor. We do not ask for changes to other medicines, and we do not claim to replace acid suppression, prokinetics, antispasmodics, thyroid or diabetes treatment, or other standard medical management.

Selected clinical references used for the absorption explanation

NIH Office of Dietary Supplements: Iron, Health Professional Fact Sheet. Iron absorption depends on chemical form, food matrix, vitamin C, calcium, phytate/polyphenols and gastric acidity; iron also interacts with levothyroxine.

Iron absorption physiology: duodenal reduction of Fe³⁺ to Fe²⁺, DMT1 uptake, ferritin storage, ferroportin export and transferrin transport.

Probiotic evidence: evidence for improved iron absorption is strain-specific. A 2025 systematic review/meta-analysis found some improvement in fractional absorption and ferritin in women of reproductive age, while haemoglobin benefit remained uncertain.

Long-term PPI use: product labelling and regulator guidance describe possible vitamin B12 and magnesium deficiency with prolonged use, and rebound acid hypersecretion after withdrawal has been described in the literature.
