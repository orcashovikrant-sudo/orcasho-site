Introduction

My Biggest Achievements: From Building Computers As A Child To Building A Biochemical Intervention System

My biggest achievements have come from repeatedly teaching myself things I was not formally trained to do, finding practical ways to turn that knowledge into something valuable, and continuing to build despite financial constraints and setbacks. My journey has taken me from washing cars and assembling computers as a school student in India to conducting research at Argonne National Laboratory, earning a Marie Skłodowska-Curie Fellowship from the European Union, publishing in an American Chemical Society journal, and building Orcasho, a biochemical intervention system that has been used across approximately 300 patients. The common thread is that I have always been interested in understanding how systems work beneath the surface. I started by taking apart computers, moved into experimental physics and chemistry, and eventually began applying the same way of thinking to human biochemistry and chronic health.

Childhood: Learning To Earn, Build And Solve Problems

My family was not financially well off, and money was a serious constraint throughout my childhood. Rather than waiting until I was older to earn money, I began finding ways to do it myself.

In sixth grade, I started washing cars during summer vacations. I washed approximately 10 to 12 cars at ₹30 per car, earning ₹300 to ₹360 for a full round of work. It was not a significant amount of money, but it taught me something early: I could identify a service people needed, learn to deliver it, and earn money independently.

By seventh grade, computers and individual hardware components were becoming more accessible in India. I started learning how computers worked and began assembling machines for neighbours and other people who wanted affordable alternatives to branded computers from companies such as HP and Compaq. At the time, branded systems could cost several times more than a carefully assembled machine.

I learned how to select and assemble components, configure computers, troubleshoot hardware, install operating systems and set up software and games. I earned money by doing this for people in my neighbourhood.

I did not learn computing through a formal course. I learned because there was a real problem to solve, people were willing to pay for the solution, and I needed to understand how the technology worked well enough to deliver it.

Around tenth grade, I also started working with Nokia retail operations. My role involved selling Nokia phones to corporate customers and explaining their technology and software capabilities. I learned to work with the Symbian operating system and configure additional software for customers.

These experiences gave me my first practical exposure to technology, customer needs, sales and the economics of building something useful. Long before I thought of myself as an entrepreneur, I was already learning to combine technical knowledge with commercial execution.

Engineering: Financing My Own Education

I entered RV College of Engineering in Bengaluru in 2006, one of India's well-regarded engineering institutions.

Even during engineering, I needed to find ways to support myself financially. I worked in contract recruitment, helping companies identify candidates and persuade professionals to move between organisations. I used the income to contribute towards my education expenses and eventually bought myself a car.

Recruitment was a different field from engineering, but the work taught me how businesses operate, how incentives influence decisions, how to communicate with people, and how to get things done when there is no established process to rely on.

However, my deeper interest was never limited to the subjects I was formally studying.

From childhood, I had been fascinated by fundamental scientific systems: the laws of nature, the behaviour of matter, and the chemistry underlying physical processes. I watched scientific documentaries and read about chemistry independently because the questions that interested me were often outside the prescribed syllabus.

I became increasingly interested in understanding why things behaved as they did, rather than simply learning how to use existing tools.

That curiosity eventually led me away from a conventional engineering career and towards experimental research.

Postgraduate Research In The United Kingdom: Building My Own Experimental System

After engineering, I pursued postgraduate studies at the University of Sussex and the University of Brighton in the United Kingdom.

During my master's research, I worked on a thesis connected to BMW and developed an experimental spiral-flow device to measure airflow inside a cylinder. I also created a MATLAB program to analyse the resulting measurements.

I was particularly interested in the experimental side of the work. I wanted to build the apparatus, understand the physical phenomenon, collect the data and develop the computational method required to interpret it.

Alongside my studies, I took on paid work to support myself. I helped international students, including Chinese students facing language barriers, with technical coursework and assignments, and worked in laboratories as an assistant to my professor.

At my peak, I earned approximately £1,800 per month through this work. That income helped me pay my rent and cover my living expenses in the UK.

This period reinforced two habits that have remained central to my work: I am comfortable learning across disciplines, and I am willing to do the unglamorous work required to make an idea function in the real world.

Winning A Marie Skłodowska-Curie Fellowship After Years Of Applications

After completing my postgraduate studies, I returned to India with the ambition of entering advanced scientific research through the Marie Skłodowska-Curie Innovative Training Network (ITN), funded by the European Union's Horizon 2020 programme.

The fellowship was highly competitive. My prospective supervisor told me that my chances were approximately 1%, given the level of competition.

I had very little money at the time. For approximately two and a half years, I worked as a recruiter, earning around ₹20,000 per month, while continuing to pursue research opportunities.

My working day often began at 3:30 a.m. Between approximately 3:30 a.m. and 7:00 a.m., I worked on research proposals for universities and laboratories participating in European research programmes. I searched for suitable projects, studied the problems researchers were trying to solve, and tailored applications to those projects.

I submitted dozens of proposals, approximately 50 to 90 by my recollection, before securing the fellowship.

I was ultimately selected for the Marie Skłodowska-Curie ITN programme, IPPAD, project 675528. I was the only Indian in my cohort of 15 early-stage researchers.

I was subsequently elected by my peers as the Early Stage Researcher Representative for the consortium, representing the researchers across the international network.

The fellowship was one of the defining achievements of my life. I had pursued it for years while working to support myself financially, and I had secured it despite being told that my chances were extremely low.

The experience taught me that access to high-level scientific research does not have to be determined by where someone starts. Persistence, independent learning and the willingness to keep improving one's work can create opportunities that initially appear inaccessible.

Research At Argonne National Laboratory And An ACS Publication

Through the fellowship, I worked across an international research network involving universities, industrial research partners and laboratories.

My research took me to Argonne National Laboratory in the United States, where I worked on synchrotron X-ray phase-contrast imaging of fuel injection. I also worked with Afton Chemical and Virginia Commonwealth University, and my research was based at the University of Erlangen-Nuremberg in Germany. The broader network included Lund University in Sweden and other European research partners.

At Argonne, I worked on experimental imaging of fuel-injection processes, examining phenomena such as cavitation, vaporisation and phase transitions in fuel sprays. This required connecting physical chemistry with experimental instrumentation, imaging and quantitative analysis.

I was particularly proud of being invited to undertake research at Argonne. Among the 15 researchers in my fellowship cohort, I was the one who received this opportunity.

During this period, I also co-authored a peer-reviewed paper published in the American Chemical Society's Journal of Chemical & Engineering Data in 2019:

Effect of Composition, Temperature, and Pressure on the Viscosities and Densities of Three Diesel Fuels.

The work examined how fuel composition and environmental conditions affect viscosity and density. It was a rigorous application of experimental science to a practical industrial problem.

These experiences shaped how I approach problems today. Physical chemistry taught me to think in terms of states, interactions, constraints and measurable changes. Experimental research taught me to build models that can be confronted with observations rather than relying entirely on intuition.

Those principles later became central to Orcasho.

Founding FlyitOut: Turning An Everyday Observation Into A Technology Business

Towards the end of 2019, my frequent international travel for research gave me an idea for a different kind of business.

As I travelled between laboratories and countries, I often carried items for other people. I realised that travellers were already moving between cities and countries, while people elsewhere needed goods transported along those same routes.

I saw an opportunity to connect the two through a peer-to-peer delivery platform. FlyitOut was designed to match people who needed items transported with travellers already heading to the relevant destination, both internationally and within India.

The business began gaining traction, including through domestic travel during the COVID period and the subsequent recovery in mobility. We attracted investor interest and began developing the technology needed to make the matching process more intelligent.

I also built a machine learning model to analyse flight paths and travel patterns, identifying routes with high passenger movement between locations. The goal was to use actual travel patterns to improve route selection and matching efficiency. This was another instance of learning by building: applying analytical thinking to a commercial problem.

However, FlyitOut eventually became entangled in a serious co-founder dispute. After I decided to remove a family-member co-founder whose contribution had become a concern, the dispute escalated into legal proceedings before the National Company Law Tribunal (NCLT) in Bengaluru. The proceedings lasted approximately two and a half years, and the company's banking access and operations were disrupted.

The legal proceedings ultimately concluded in my favour. Nevertheless, the prolonged dispute made it impossible to continue operating FlyitOut as intended, and the company was wound down.

This was one of the most difficult periods of my entrepreneurial life. I learned that a company's survival depends not only on its product, technology or market demand, but also on founder alignment, accountability and governance. Trust alone is not a substitute for demonstrated execution and clearly defined responsibilities.

The experience changed how I evaluate partnerships. I became much more deliberate about ownership, decision-making authority, operational accountability and the risks created by dependence on another individual.

Importantly, I did not stop building during this period. I continued developing the biochemical work that would become Orcasho.

Orcasho: Turning Years Of Independent Biochemical Work Into A Company

My work on digestive health did not begin as a startup idea.

During my scientific training, I had become interested in the role of chemistry, ionic equilibria and environmental conditions in determining how substances behave. I began applying that way of thinking to digestive biochemistry and experimenting with supplement-based interventions, initially through my own work and by helping people I knew.

The underlying question was whether digestive dysfunction could be approached as a changing biochemical system, rather than treating every symptom as an isolated problem.

I continued exploring this while building FlyitOut. People I had been helping over several years began paying for continued support, and I saw that the work could become a sustainable business.

In January 2022, I formally incorporated Orcasho Scientific Solutions Private Limited.

We received our first patient payment from someone who had known my work for three to four years. We subsequently raised approximately $120,000 from family and friends, giving me the resources to develop the business and its treatment system. We launched commercially in January 2023.

Orcasho began with a practical objective: provide adaptive digestive-health interventions that could be reassessed as a patient's condition changed, without requiring the patient to reorganise their entire life around a restrictive protocol.

We developed a system in which patients are reassessed in recurring treatment cycles, with prescriptions adjusted according to their symptoms, medication context, food patterns and prior responses. The 20-day adaptation interval became a central part of the operating model.

What started as a biochemical hypothesis became a live operating system used with real patients.

Building A Real-World Dataset Through Repeated Prescription And Feedback Cycles

One of my most important achievements with Orcasho has been creating a longitudinal dataset that connects what was prescribed to what happened afterwards.

We have managed approximately 300 patients and accumulated more than 1,700 prescription-to-feedback records through repeated treatment cycles.

Each cycle captures a patient state, the intervention selected, the subsequent feedback and the decision about what to change next. This creates a record of interventions and observed responses in the context of patients' actual lives, rather than isolated measurements collected at a single point in time.

The system tracks changing symptoms and relevant patient information over time. We have represented 101 compounds within our intervention framework, giving us a common structure for analysing candidate interventions and their relationships with the observed patient state.

The commercial results matter because they show that this is not merely a research concept.

Typical patient tenure is approximately 9 to 18 months, with some patients remaining considerably longer.

112 of 291 patients have returned, generating 263 retention orders, which is a 90.4% retention-order rate relative to first-time patient orders. Retention orders account for 47.5% of all orders.

The achievement is not simply the number of patients. It is that the company has accumulated repeated observations linking interventions to subsequent responses, and that this record is being used to inform future intervention decisions.

The business has also continued generating revenue without paid advertising. From April 2026 through September 2026, we spent nothing on marketing, including paid advertising. Revenue was approximately $2,700 in Q2 2026 and approximately $2,700 in Q3 2026, primarily supported by returning patients and referrals.

We have built this operating history with limited capital and a small team. The customer relationships, repeat purchases and accumulated records have been generated through the actual delivery of the service.

Teaching Myself Software Development And Building The Product Without A Technical Co-Founder

One of my most personally significant achievements has been learning to build the software underlying Orcasho myself.

I am a physical chemist, not a formally trained software engineer. When our engineering resources became constrained, I had to take direct responsibility for developing and maintaining the product.

I taught myself HTML, JavaScript and Python and began building the operational software, patient dashboards, prescription-feedback workflows and data-processing systems. I now use Claude as a coding and development partner, but I define the architecture, data structures, biochemical framework, decision logic and modelling approach.

The current system has two principal components.

The first is the operational application, built around HTML and JavaScript and connected to Firebase. It supports patient operations, consultations, dashboards and the day-to-day treatment workflow.

The second is the proprietary data-processing and simulation system, which runs locally in Python. It processes longitudinal prescription and feedback data, analyses recurring prescription-response patterns and prepares empirical inputs for the biochemical simulation.

I built the core system myself. I did not hire a replacement technical co-founder to build it for me, and I did not need to become a conventional software engineer before beginning to develop the product.

The more important achievement is what this software enables. It connects the operational reality of treating patients to the data and modelling system used to analyse and adapt interventions.

This experience changed my understanding of what I could build. I had previously worked with experimental equipment and computational models in physical chemistry. I was now applying the same systems-oriented approach to a software product, using modern development tools to turn scientific concepts into an operational system.

Building Orcasho's Proprietary Intervention-Response And Simulation Architecture

The next major achievement was recognising that the prescription-feedback record could support something more powerful than a treatment workflow.

As the dataset grew, we began structuring the relationship between patient state, intervention, observed response and the next intervention. We then built a biochemical framework that could represent candidate interventions and examine their behaviour across the digestive tract.

Our current architecture models four pH-defined digestive gates.

The stomach represents the strongly acidic environment and the chemical behaviour of candidate compounds.

The duodenum represents the transition from gastric acidity towards a more alkaline environment, including bicarbonate and bile.

The small intestine represents the chemical context surrounding solubility, interactions and uptake.

The colon represents downstream conditions, including the context in which microbial activity may affect compounds and their products.

The system represents interventions and patient states across biochemical dimensions. Candidate prescriptions are simulated through these four gates, and cosine similarity quantifies how closely a simulated chemical trajectory aligns with the target state.

The model is not simply a list of ingredients or a generic recommendation engine. It connects a mechanistic chemical representation to the longitudinal record of real prescriptions and patient feedback.

The data pipeline includes the patient's observed state and relevant history, the prescription selected for that state, feedback collected after the intervention, historical prescription frequencies and associated response patterns, Random Forest analysis of recurring relationships, Python-based processing of empirical inputs, simulation of candidate interventions through the four digestive gates, cosine-similarity scoring against the target trajectory, and selection and reassessment of the next intervention.

The empirical prescription-frequency weights come from the accumulated prescription-response record. The simulation combines those observed inputs with the mechanistic framework to compare candidate interventions.

The detailed encoding, thresholds and scoring methods are proprietary. The broader architecture is described at https://orcasho.com/interventionlayer/.

This is an important milestone because it moves the company from an adaptive treatment operation towards a reusable intervention-response technology.

The Larger Opportunity: An Intervention Layer That Can Extend Beyond Digestive Health

The most significant insight emerging from Orcasho is that the underlying system may have applications beyond the original digestive-health business.

Different industries already develop valuable assets. Pharmaceutical companies develop molecules. AI drug-discovery companies identify candidate compounds. Microbiome companies develop microbial strains. Supplement companies develop formulations. Diagnostics companies generate measurements.

What is often missing is a structured way to interpret those assets within a changing human context, alongside other interventions, food, medications and physiological conditions.

Orcasho is being built around that missing layer.

The framework takes an intervention, represents it in a common chemical language, evaluates its behaviour across the four digestive gates, places it in the context of a changing patient state and connects the resulting hypothesis to observed intervention-response data.

The initial application is digestive health, where we already have a live operating system and a longitudinal patient dataset. The broader objective is to develop a framework that can be applied to external interventions, including drugs, molecules, microbial strains, supplements and food constituents.

The potential significance is that this framework is not tied to one ingredient brand or one fixed treatment protocol. Its underlying language is based on chemical properties, pH, ionic interactions and the changing environment through which interventions pass.

Wherever the relevant biochemical processes apply, the framework can potentially be extended to new interventions and use cases. That gives us a path from a focused healthcare business towards a technology layer that could serve chronic-care organisations and life-science companies.

We are pursuing this in stages: continue expanding the patient base, deepen the longitudinal intervention-response dataset, improve the simulation system, and establish B2B applications in the United States.

The intervention layer was not the original business plan. It emerged from years of operating the treatment system and accumulating real-world data. That is what makes the opportunity compelling to me: the broader technology grew out of a problem we were already solving, rather than being designed in isolation.

What Connects These Achievements

Looking back, my journey has not followed a conventional path, from washing cars to building a biochemical simulation framework on top of real patient data.

The pattern is consistent: I learn by working directly on difficult problems, build the tools needed to solve them, and continue until the work produces something that can operate in the real world.

My long-term ambition is to build technology that helps us understand and predict how interventions behave in complex biological systems. I see chemistry as a foundational language for this work, and I believe the intervention-response layer we are developing can become much more than a digestive-health product.

The achievement I am most proud of is that I have taken an idea rooted in fundamental chemistry, turned it into a functioning patient-facing business, and begun building a proprietary computational system around the real-world data generated by that business. I have done this without a traditional software-engineering background, with limited capital, and without a technical co-founder.
